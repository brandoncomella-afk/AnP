PART 2 · EXCITATION–CONTRACTION COUPLING

From spike to calcium

Steps light up in order each time a spike fires (with “Each spike triggers a twitch” on). Tap any step to highlight it.

  1. Nerve impulse arrivesA motor neuron action potential reaches the axon terminal.
  2. ACh is releasedCa²⁺ enters the terminal and vesicles dump acetylcholine into the synaptic cleft.
  3. End plate depolarizesACh opens receptor channels on the motor end plate; Na⁺ rushes in.
  4. Muscle AP spreadsThe action potential races along the sarcolemma and down the T-tubules.
  5. SR gates openVoltage sensors (DHP receptors) in the T-tubules open ryanodine receptors in the sarcoplasmic reticulum.
  6. Ca²⁺ floods outCalcium pours into the sarcoplasm and binds troponin.
  7. Sites uncoveredTroponin shifts tropomyosin off the myosin-binding sites on actin.
  8. Cross-bridges cycleMyosin heads bind, pull thin filaments toward the M line, release, and repeat.
  9. RelaxationAcetylcholinesterase destroys ACh; SERCA pumps spend ATP to return Ca²⁺ to the SR; tropomyosin re-covers the sites.

CHECK YOURSELF

Predict, then test

Write a prediction first, run the experiment, then open the explanation.

Raise the stimulus a little at a time from 0. Once a spike appears, does a stronger stimulus make a taller spike?

No. Below threshold you only get a small graded bump that fades. Once threshold is crossed, positive feedback (more depolarization opens more Na⁺ channels) produces a full spike of about the same height every time. This is the all-or-none principle. A stronger stimulus mostly makes the spike start sooner.

Fire a double pulse with a 6 ms gap. Why does the second pulse fail? Then try a 12 ms gap at 10 and at 20 µA/cm².

Right after a spike, the Na⁺ inactivation gates are closed, and no stimulus can reopen those channels until they reset. That is the absolute refractory period. At a 12 ms gap, 10 µA/cm² still fails but 20 µA/cm² fires a second spike: the membrane can fire again, but only with a stronger push, because some Na⁺ channels are still inactivated and K⁺ gates are still open. That is the relative refractory period. (This squid-axon model runs cold, so its refractory periods are longer than in a human neuron.)

Block Na⁺ channels, then stimulate hard. What happens, and why would that matter for a nerve?

No spike forms, because the rising phase depends on Na⁺ rushing in. Tetrodotoxin (pufferfish toxin) and local anesthetics like lidocaine work by blocking voltage-gated Na⁺ channels, so signals never reach the muscle.

Block K⁺ channels and fire a spike. Which phase changes most?

Repolarization. Without K⁺ flowing out, the voltage drifts down slowly as Na⁺ channels inactivate and leak currents act. There is no undershoot below rest.

During a contraction, which bands get shorter and which stay the same?

The I band and H zone shrink. The A band stays 1.60 µm because it is the length of the thick filaments, and filaments don’t change length. That observation in the 1950s is what led to the sliding filament model.

Hold Ca²⁺ high, let the sarcomere shorten, then turn ATP off and release the calcium. Why does it stay locked?

Myosin needs a new ATP to let go of actin. Without ATP, attached heads stay in the bound (rigor) state and the sarcomere can’t relax. SERCA pumps also need ATP, so Ca²⁺ isn’t cleared. This is why muscles stiffen in rigor mortis.

Compare one twitch with holding Ca²⁺ high. How does the body hold a steady contraction?

A single spike releases a brief pulse of Ca²⁺, so the sarcomere shortens and then relaxes. When spikes come fast enough, Ca²⁺ never gets pumped away and the contraction is sustained. This is tetanus (the physiology term, not the disease).

Model notes. The voltage trace solves the Hodgkin–Huxley equations for the squid giant axon (rest ≈ −65 mV, threshold ≈ −55 mV). Textbooks often use −70 mV for neurons; skeletal muscle fibers rest closer to −90 mV, but the shape and logic of the spike are the same. Time is slowed, and the three parts are not on the same time scale. The sarcomere uses textbook-typical dimensions: thick filaments 1.6 µm, thin filaments 1.0 µm per side, resting sarcomere 2.6 µm.
